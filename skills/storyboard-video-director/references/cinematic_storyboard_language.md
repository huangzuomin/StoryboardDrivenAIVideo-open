# Cinematic Storyboard Language

Use this file when writing annotated storyboard prompts, Beat plans, or image-agent tasks that need professional camera language.

Sources distilled: StudioBinder camera shots and storyboarding guides, Adobe camera shots/angles and transitions guides, Boords storyboard template guidance, Learn About Film camera position notes, ASC lens discussion, and Bordwell/Thompson film-term glossary material.

## Core Formula

Use this order inside each panel description:

```text
[shot size], [camera angle], [lens / depth], [composition], [camera movement], [action], [lighting/mood], [transition or annotation]
```

Example:

```text
Medium close-up, slight low angle, 50mm lens, subject framed on left third with heavy negative space, slow push-in as she realizes the truth, hard side light, cut on eye movement.
```

## Shot Sizes

- `extreme wide shot`: geography, scale, isolation, establishing world.
- `wide shot`: full body plus environment, action clarity.
- `full shot`: head-to-toe posture, costume, readable silhouette.
- `medium wide / cowboy shot`: body language with facial readability.
- `medium shot`: waist-up human attention and dialogue.
- `medium close-up`: emotion with shoulder/body context.
- `close-up`: decision, reaction, face, hands, or object emphasis.
- `extreme close-up`: symbolic detail, eyes, fingers, clue, texture.
- `insert`: continuity detail, prop, screen, hand action.
- `cutaway`: contextual detail outside the main action.
- `over-the-shoulder`: relationship, dialogue, POV pressure.
- `POV`: subjective experience.
- `reaction shot`: emotional consequence.

## Camera Angles

- `eye-level`: neutral, grounded, observational.
- `high angle`: vulnerability, exposure, smallness.
- `low angle`: power, threat, awe, dominance.
- `overhead / bird's-eye`: pattern, strategy, choreography, geography.
- `worm's-eye`: extreme intimidation or surreal scale.
- `Dutch angle`: instability and disorientation; use sparingly.
- `profile`: graphic silhouette or emotional distance.
- `three-quarter angle`: natural cinematic body/face dimension.
- `frontal`: confrontation, symmetry, directness.

## Camera Movement

- `pan`: rotate left/right to reveal or follow lateral action.
- `tilt`: rotate up/down to reveal height, body, or object.
- `push-in / dolly in`: pressure, realization, intimacy.
- `pull-back / dolly out`: isolation, reveal, emotional withdrawal.
- `tracking / trucking`: follow motion and preserve momentum.
- `crane / jib`: rise/fall for scale, reveal, or transition.
- `handheld`: urgency, instability, documentary pressure.
- `Steadicam / gimbal`: smooth immersive following.
- `whip pan`: fast energetic transition or surprise.
- `zoom`: stylized attention or surveillance; distinguish from dolly.
- `rack focus`: redirect attention between planes.

Always define a movement start and end state:

```text
Blue arrow: camera starts low behind the chair, tracks frame-right with the chase, and ends in a low-angle close-up of the impact.
```

## Lens Language

- `16mm-24mm wide-angle`: dynamic movement, spatial distortion, cramped rooms, chase energy.
- `28mm`: active wide storytelling with readable environment.
- `35mm`: natural wide human drama, versatile storyboard lens note.
- `50mm`: intimate natural perspective.
- `85mm+ telephoto`: compression, portrait isolation, surveillance distance.
- `macro`: tactile detail or clue insert.
- `shallow depth of field`: emotional isolation; avoid for complex action geography.
- `deep focus`: action clarity, multi-plane blocking, hazard geography.
- `split-diopter style`: dual attention in foreground and background.

Do not confuse shot size and lens: a wide shot can use a long lens, and a close-up can use a wide lens.

## Composition

- `rule of thirds`: balanced readable framing.
- `center framing`: iconic, formal, confrontational.
- `leading lines`: guide attention and depth.
- `negative space`: loneliness, threat, anticipation.
- `foreground framing`: depth, concealment, voyeurism.
- `silhouette`: graphic drama and strong shape readability.
- `layered staging`: foreground / midground / background story.
- `frame within frame`: entrapment, separation, mirror/window logic.
- `look room`: leave space in direction of gaze or movement.
- `screen direction`: state left-to-right or right-to-left continuity for action.

## Transitions

- `hard cut`: default direct continuity.
- `match cut`: connect by shape, action, composition, light, or posture.
- `match on action`: cut through the same motion.
- `jump cut`: fragmented time, anxiety, stylized compression.
- `cross dissolve`: time passage, memory, softness.
- `fade in / fade out`: beginning, ending, chapter break.
- `dip to white/black`: flash, rupture, punctuation.
- `wipe`: stylized scene change or motion-driven transition.
- `smash cut`: abrupt contrast or shock.
- `J-cut / L-cut`: audio leads or lags image.

## Annotation Conventions

Each professional storyboard panel should carry only the notes needed to direct the shot:

- panel label
- shot size
- angle or lens note
- subject action
- camera movement
- composition priority
- lighting or hazard direction
- sound, vocal, or emotional note when rhythm matters
- transition or timing note if needed

Use the project color system:

- red = subject movement
- blue = camera movement
- green = framing / composition
- orange = lighting / danger / signal
- purple = emotion / vocal / impact / sound
- black = panel label, lens note, short shot note

## Common Mistakes

- Writing `cinematic` without shot size, lens, camera movement, and composition.
- Stacking incoherent camera movement: `static handheld tracking push-in zoom`.
- Forgetting screen direction in action sequences.
- Cropping full-body choreography when movement clarity matters.
- Using Dutch angle in every panel.
- Asking for shallow focus when environment geography must remain readable.
- Giving camera motion without start and end framing.
- Letting annotations dominate the drawing.
